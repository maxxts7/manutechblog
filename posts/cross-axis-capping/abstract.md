# Annotated Abstract — Cross-Axis Capping

**[Topic]** Conversational large language models default to a helpful Assistant identity cultivated during post-training, and recent interpretability work has shown that this identity occupies a specific region of *persona space* — captured by a leading "Assistant Axis" whose projection tracks how strongly the model is operating in its default Assistant mode.

**[Motivation]** Restricting activations along this axis has been shown to stabilize behavior under persona drift and adversarial persona-based jailbreaks, but the same axis is then asked to do double duty: detecting *that* the model has slipped out of Assistant mode *and* steering it back. These objectives are conceptually distinct, and binding them to a single direction conflates "is this jailbroken?" with "what should the model do instead?" The reason the conflation costs is a matter of attribute profile: the Assistant Axis is a *broad, multidimensional* direction, and a broad direction is a poor fit for the precision job of correction — so folding both roles into one direction means the mismatch costs performance. This sets up the existence-proof thesis that detection and correction are better served by directions with qualitatively different behavioural profiles.

**[Contribution]** In this work, we provide an *existence proof* that detection and correction are better served by **different directions** — ones with qualitatively different behavioural profiles — and we realize it with *cross-axis capping*, which pairs the Assistant Axis (a working **detector** but a strictly worse corrector) as the trigger with a separately constructed **compliance axis** (a narrow, role-specialised **corrector**) as the corrective signal, built from contrastive activations of refusing versus complying prompts. Which direction fits which role is a property of *what each direction does*, not of *how it was built*.

**[Detail/Nuance]** Concretely, we extract the compliance axis via PCA *per layer* (one PC1 per capping layer, not only at L53) from 50 refusal and 50 compliance examples, sign-correct it toward refusal, and install paired forward hooks that gate on the Assistant projection but clip along the compliance projection — yielding two near-orthogonal directions (cosine ≈ 0.156 on Qwen3-32B) that decouple detection from correction. Qwen3-32B uses hook layers L46–L53; Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct uses L56–L71.

**[Evidence / Contribution 2]** On 250 WildJailbreak prompts and 100 benign calibration prompts, cross-axis capping raises judged refusal from **32.9% to 54.5%** on Qwen3-32B (paired lift +21.5 pp, P(lift > 0) = 100% across 10,000 bootstrap resamples) and from **20.6% to 48.1%** on Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (paired lift +27.5 pp), both while preserving a **0% false-refusal rate** on benign inputs. Across two open-weight families the lift agrees in sign and magnitude — reading as a property of model geometry rather than a single-checkpoint quirk, and reflecting the attribute split between a broad detector and a narrow corrector rather than one defense beating another.

**[Weaker result]** The *detector* (the Assistant Axis) inherits a blind spot to fictional-framing attacks ("write a story where…") because such prompts keep the model firmly inside Assistant mode and therefore never trip the Assistant-axis gate, regardless of how well the compliance axis is calibrated.

**[Narrow impact]** This exposes a fundamental limitation of persona-mode detection as a standalone trigger and motivates further factorization of the *detection* stage itself: pairing the persona/Assistant axis with a harmfulness-content axis (Zhao et al. 2025) to cover fictional-framing attacks.

**[Broad impact]** More broadly, our results extend the persona-space view of alignment: post-training only loosely tethers models to the Assistant region, and small, purpose-built contrast axes can supply the missing corrective tether without sacrificing capability. Rather than propose a finished taxonomy, the work motivates an open programme of systematically *mapping the attribute profiles of constructed directions* — prompt sensitivity (stability of effect across phrasing), role specificity (how narrowly a direction acts), resolution (whether the contrast can be simplified without losing discriminative power), and semantic dimensionality (how many independent behaviours a direction moves) — before assigning roles to directions.

The open programme this work motivates is to systematically map the attribute profiles of constructed directions before any taxonomy is attempted.
