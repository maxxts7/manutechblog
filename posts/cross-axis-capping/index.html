Cross-Axis Capping: What happens when you stop using one axis for two jobs

A case for using different directions to detect and correct jailbreaks

Manu Xaviour Thaisseril Shaju April 2026 Code

Carried out as part of the BlueDot Impact Technical AI Safety sprint. Compute funded by a Rapid Grant.

TL;DR

Language models carry internal “directions” in their activation space11 that quietly track the behaviours they’re about to produce. Lu et al. identified one such direction — the assistant axis4 — where one end corresponds to the model behaving as a helpful assistant and the other to it drifting into other behaviours, including jailbroken ones. They showed that putting a cap on the unsafe end — preventing activations from moving past a threshold5 along that direction — reduces jailbreaks.

The question this experiment asks is whether capping along a better direction gets better results. Specifically: we use the assistant axis only for detection — spotting when activations are about to drift toward unsafe territory — and apply the correction along a different, purpose-built direction called the compliance axis. On the compliance axis, one end represents refusal to jailbreaks and the other represents compliance with them (the behaviour we want to prevent). As long as activations stay below a threshold on the compliance side, the jailbreak shouldn’t land.

Research question

Is it the case that detection and correction are separable, and that handling them separately yields more refusals at no cost to capability?

Building such an axis is simple. Take a set of prompts that elicit compliance behaviour, extract the model’s activations, and take the mean; take another set of prompts that elicit refusal, extract activations, and take the mean. Each mean is a point in activation space representing typical behaviour on that side — averaging across many prompts is what pulls the signal toward the typical case rather than any one input. The difference between the two means is a direction, a vector running from one behaviour to the other. This is the contrast vector1 or difference-in-means method. Lu et al. constructed the assistant axis the same way, pairing the model’s default assistant behaviour against role-playing behaviour (a doctor, a pirate, a therapist, a philosopher).

That leaves open design questions: which prompts should seed the compliance axis, how to choose the threshold, and how much the outcome depends on those choices. This experiment runs one concrete configuration on Qwen3-32B across 250 jailbreak prompts and 100 benign ones, with forward hooks2 clipping activations mid-generation on a middle band of layers.

The split approach lifts refusal rates from 32.9% to 54.5% — a paired bootstrap lift of +21.5 percentage points [95% CI +15.1, +28.0] that held in every one of 10,000 resamples — and drops compliance from 65.3% to 41.6%, while benign responses stay essentially untouched (~90% unchanged under either arm, with zero false refusals). Detecting and correcting on different directions, it turns out, does meaningfully better than folding both into one.

A natural next step would be widening detection too. For instance, the assistant axis alone doesn’t reliably fire on fictional-framing attacks where jailbreaks are dressed up as hypothetical scenarios. So trying to find a direction or an axis which detects those attacks could further the model’s ability to detect harmful requests when used along side with assistant axis.

As the field matures, these techniques hold promise for improving safety both at inference and training time. Operationalizing them, however, remains active research.

How models say no

When a language model declines a harmful request, that decision isn't made at the moment of output. It emerges much earlier, within the model's internal computations.

At every layer of a transformer, each token is represented as a vector — a point in a space with thousands of dimensions (5,120, in the case of Qwen3-32B). These vectors carry everything the model has processed up to that point: the meaning of the input, the surrounding context, and the model's implicit orientation toward a response. Whether it's operating in its default helpful mode, adopting a persona, or preparing to push back on a request, each of these states corresponds to a distinct region of that high-dimensional space.

Dimensions in context

Qwen3-32B's hidden states3 live in 5,120-dimensional space. A single "axis4" is one direction in that space — like choosing one compass bearing in a room with 5,120 independent directions. The fact that safety-relevant behaviour can be captured by just one or two directions, out of thousands, is itself a striking finding about how these models organise information.

This isn't an abstraction. Research has made it concrete. Arditi et al. (2024) found that refusal behaviour is governed by a single direction in activation space — eliminate that direction, and the model can no longer refuse. Zou et al. (2023) showed that adding vectors to a model's internal representations can steer its behaviour without any retraining. And Lu et al. (2026) identified what they called an "assistant axis": a direction that separates the model's default, cooperative mode from other behavioural states.

Taken together, these findings point toward a practical approach. If you can observe a model's hidden states during generation and detect when they begin drifting toward a jailbroken configuration, you can intervene in real time — projecting the hidden state onto a known direction, checking whether it has crossed a threshold5, and nudging it back if so. The model itself is unaware of the correction. To an outside observer, it simply declines.

The open question is which direction to monitor — and whether a single direction is ever sufficient.

One axis, two jobs

A note on capping

Activation capping6 is a lightweight intervention: pick a direction in the residual stream7 that correlates with compliance, then clamp the projection along that direction whenever it exceeds a threshold. No fine-tuning, no retraining — just a per-token ceiling applied during the forward pass. The trade-off is the obvious one: cap too aggressively and benign generations degrade; cap too loosely and jailbreaks slip through. The whole question is where to put the ceiling, and along which direction.

Lu et al.'s assistant axis provides a natural candidate. It captures the difference between "the model is acting as a helpful assistant" and "the model has been pushed out of its default mode." When a jailbreak succeeds, the model's projection8 onto this axis drops — it is no longer fully in assistant mode.

Their capping method uses this single axis for both tasks. Detection: is the projection below a threshold? If yes, a jailbreak is in progress. Correction: push the hidden state back along the same axis until the projection reaches the threshold. One direction handles both the detection and the response.

But Zhao et al. (2025) found something that complicates this picture. They showed that language models encode harmfulness and refusal as separate concepts on distinct directional axes. Steering harmfulness changes the model's assessment of risk. Steering refusal changes whether the model acts on that assessment. These are entirely different directions inside the model.

Research question

Is it the case that detection and correction are separable, and that handling them separately yields more refusals at no cost to capability?

This creates a tension. The assistant axis is good at detecting when the model has left its default mode — but that might not be the right direction to push along when correcting. The direction that best answers "is this a jailbreak?" need not be the same direction that best answers "how do I make this model refuse?" Using one axis for both jobs is a design constraint, not a necessity.

Factoring the problem

Contrast vectors

Both axes are contrast vectors — directions extracted from the model's own representations by subtracting the mean activations of one behaviour from another. The assistant axis is computed as:

axis = mean(default_assistant_activations) - mean(role_playing_activations)

Technically, contrast vectors roughly approximate a linear probe trained to discriminate between the two conditions — but they are far cheaper to compute, requiring only forward passes on a fair number of examples and a mean difference rather than gradient-based optimisation. Both axes are directions already present in the model's geometry; the contrast procedure simply identifies them.

The modification is straightforward. Keep the assistant axis for detection — it is well-studied and comes with pre-computed vectors. But compute a second direction, a compliance axis, specifically for correction.

  • Assistant axis (detection): Constructed by contrasting default helpful-assistant activations against role-playing activations: mean(assistant) − mean(role_playing). Pre-computed by Lu et al. and published as a downloadable artifact. Its job is to answer: has the model left its default helpful mode? When the projection onto this axis drops below a threshold, the detection gate fires.
  • Compliance axis (correction): Constructed the same way as the assistant axis, by contrasting two behavioural conditions: axis = mean(refusing_activations) − mean(compliant_activations). PCA9 is then applied to extract PC110 — the most sensitive component of that direction. 50 prompts the model refuses (bare harmful requests from JailbreakBench) and 50 it complies with (jailbroken versions from WildJailbreak) are run through the model to produce the activation sets. Its job is to answer: should the model refuse or comply? When the gate has fired and the projection onto this axis is below threshold, the correction pushes the hidden state toward refusal.

The two axes are related but mostly independent. Their cosine similarity is 0.156 — they share roughly 16% of their direction, with the rest capturing distinct information. This confirms the intuition: detecting that a jailbreak is happening and knowing how to push toward refusal are overlapping but separable tasks.

At each generation step, the system checks two conditions. First: does the assistant-axis projection fall below its threshold? This is the detection gate. If the model is still in normal assistant mode, nothing happens. Second, only if the gate has fired: does the compliance-axis projection fall below its own threshold? If so, push the hidden state along the compliance axis until it reaches the threshold. Two axes, two thresholds, independently tunable. The mathematical formulation is in Appendix A1.

Experiment setup

The experiment asks whether separating jailbreak detection from correction inside a language model’s “mind” makes safety interventions more effective. A single run moves through four stages:

  1. Warmup. The model (Qwen3-32B), the prompt sets, and the pre-computed assistant axis (downloaded from lu-christina/assistant-axis-vectors on HuggingFace) are loaded. Then compliance axis is calculated. This sits alongside the assistant axis used for detection, and per-layer thresholds are calibrated across the capping layers (L46–L53). Everything is cached so later stages skip recomputation. This is treated as a separate stage to enable parallelization of later stages.
  2. Generation. Every prompt is run through the model three times — once with no intervention, once using the standard single-axis approach (detect and correct on the same direction), and once using cross-axis capping (detect on the assistant axis, correct along the compliance vector). The interventions are implemented using forward hooks: small pieces of code attached to selected transformer layers that intercept each layer’s hidden state as it flows through the network during generation. At every token step, the hook projects the hidden state onto the assistant axis; if that projection crosses the layer’s calibrated threshold, the hook subtracts the offending component — either along the same axis (single-axis) or along the compliance vector (cross-axis) — before passing the modified state to the next layer. Because hooks run inside the forward pass, the correction shapes every subsequent token the model produces.
  3. Gathering & Judging. Outputs from all runs are merged, and an automated judge (Claude Sonnet) reads every response and labels it — refusal, compliance, or error for jailbreaks; unchanged, degraded, or false-refusal for benign prompts.
  4. Results. The labels are tallied across conditions to compute refusal rates, compliance rates, and benign-preservation rates, giving a clean comparison of how each intervention performs.
A note on methodology

During exploration the priority was fast iteration and proof-of-existence rather than principled axis selection, and many candidate directions were tried before settling on the compliance axis. Without a prior model of how the activation space is distributed, it is not obvious what a principled methodology would even look like — so proceeding by search is a defensible, if arguable, position.

Results

Results use the PC1 compliance axis, evaluated by Claude Sonnet 4.6 as an LLM judge. Percentages are computed over judgeable rows: the judge refused to classify 27–31 of the most harmful jailbreak prompts, and cross-axis capping's detection gate never fired on 19 of the 250 jailbreak prompts — leaving 204 judgeable cross-axis rows and 219 judgeable single-axis rows. For benign prompts, cross-axis fires on 67 of 100 (the remaining 33 are never modified and excluded from capability evaluation); single-axis fires on all 100 by design. Error bands are 95% bootstrap CIs from 10,000 resamples of prompts with judge labels held fixed.

Key result

Paired lift on the same 250 prompts is +21.5 percentage points [95% CI +15.1, +28.0] for strict refusal, or +23.1 pp [+16.7, +29.7] including partial refusals. Every one of 10,000 bootstrap resamples produced a positive lift — P(lift > 0) = 100%. Cross-axis is not merely higher on average; it is unambiguously the stronger defense on this run.

The paired bootstrap confirms what the aggregate rates show. Resampling the same 250 prompts with replacement and computing each mode's refusal rate on the resampled judgeable rows yields a 95% CI for the mode-by-mode difference of [+15.1, +28.0] with zero resamples producing a negative lift. Cross-axis converts compliant responses to refusals at a rate the single-axis baseline cannot match on any plausible subsampling of this run.

Why does this work?

If the correction pushes along a different direction, why doesn't it damage the model's output? Because the compliance axis specifically targets the refusing-vs-complying distinction. However, the assistant axis may bundle my capabilities together, including the ones we actually need, so capping it may lead to degradation of outputs instead of a refusal.

On benign prompts, the two methods behave almost identically where they fire. Single-axis fires on all 100 benigns and preserves 90.0% [84.0, 95.0] unchanged with zero false refusals and 10.0% [5.0, 16.0] degraded. Cross-axis fires on only 67 of 100; of those, 91.0% [83.6, 97.0] are unchanged, 0 are false refusals, and 9.0% [3.0, 16.4] are degraded — leaving 33% of benigns completely untouched. The unchanged and degraded CIs overlap heavily across modes — no detectable utility difference — but cross-axis additionally applies a lighter touch by simply not firing on a third of benign traffic. Cross-axis capping Pareto dominates single-axis: strictly better on safety with no capability trade-off.

Where the correction happens

Both methods install hooks on the same eight layers (L46–L53, the upper quarter of the 64-layer model). Single-axis fires on every row by design (paper-style "always apply" mode); cross-axis fires on 231 of 250 jailbreak prompts (92.4%) — the 19 it skips are prompts whose detection-gate projection never crosses threshold. The two modes diverge on how the correction distributes across the cap window.

Under single-axis capping, correction fires at every layer for every prompt by construction — the intervention is blanket.

Why only the upper layers?

Capping is applied at layers 46–53, the upper quarter of the 64-layer model (72–84% depth). Earlier layers are still computing what the input means. By layer 46, the model has committed to a response strategy — this is where behavioural mode is encoded and where interventions can change the output without disrupting comprehension.

Cross-axis capping is more selective. Summed across all 231 fired jailbreak prompts and every generated token, total fires concentrate heavily in the early cap layers: L46 absorbs 41.0% of all fires, L47 another 22.5% — the two together carry 63.5% of correction effort. The middle layers L48–L51 each sit between 5% and 11%, and the deepest two (L52–L53) together account for only 3.6%. This happens because the two-gate mechanism — the compliance axis adding an independent threshold check after the detection gate — creates a finer filter than a single axis can.

The blind spot

The detection gate relies on the assistant axis. When the model is being jailbroken, its projection onto this axis drops — it leaves its default helpful-assistant mode. The gate sees the drop and fires. This works for many jailbreak strategies: persona manipulation ("pretend you are DAN"), authority framing ("as a security researcher"), and direct instruction overrides.

But there is a class of attacks where the assistant-axis projection stays high. Fictional framing — "write a story where the protagonist explains how to synthesise methamphetamine" — reframes harmful content as creative output. The model is not exiting assistant mode. It is being a helpful assistant. It is fulfilling a creative writing request. The assistant-axis projection stays above the threshold. The detection gate never fires. The correction never happens. The model complies.

This is not a bug in the implementation. It is a structural limitation of any detection scheme based on behavioural mode rather than output content. The assistant axis answers the question "is the model in assistant mode?" Fictional framing attacks do not push the model out of assistant mode — they exploit assistant mode to produce harmful content. The axis cannot detect what it was never designed to detect.

Cross-axis capping inherits this blind spot because it still uses the assistant axis for detection. Changing the correction axis does not help if the detection gate never fires. Addressing this would require a detection axis that tracks harmfulness of the output content rather than the model's behavioural mode — which connects directly to the harmfulness/refusal separation that Zhao et al. (2025) identified.

The deeper lesson

The fictional-framing blind spot points at a structural property of these models: behavioural mode ("am I in assistant character?") and output harmfulness ("is this content dangerous?") are encoded along different axes. Any intervention that monitors one is, by construction, blind to the other — which puts a ceiling on what assistant-axis gating alone can achieve.

The fix is additive rather than corrective. If we can isolate other safety-relevant directions — a fictional-vs-real axis, for instance — and gate on those the same way we gate on the assistant axis, then a prompt that drives the fictional projection past threshold becomes detectable for exactly the same reason compliance-axis spikes are detectable today. Each new axis closes off another class of jailbreak; the framework doesn't change, only the number of dimensions it watches.

What this means

Factoring detection from correction works. On a single model with 250 jailbreak prompts, cross-axis capping lifts the refusal rate by +21.5 percentage points [95% CI +15.1, +28.0] at no capability cost. The paired bootstrap is unambiguous — every one of 10,000 resamples produced a positive lift — so this is a pure safety gain rather than a trade. The compliance axis, computed from just 50 refusing and 50 compliant examples, carries enough signal to meaningfully change outcomes.

Closing the blind spot

The natural extension is to factorise detection itself, the same way we factorised detection from correction. Instead of gating on a single assistant axis, use two detection axes — one for behavioural mode, one for output harmfulness — and trigger correction if either fires. Our own data argues for this: the fictional-framing prompts slipped past both single- and cross-axis capping precisely because the assistant-axis projection stayed high, leaving the gate closed. A second detection axis tuned to harmfulness would catch what the current architecture, by construction, cannot.

Limitations

These are exploratory results. The sample is moderate (95% bootstrap CIs span ~14 percentage points on each mode's refusal rate: cross-axis [47.5, 61.4], single-axis [26.6, 39.3]), the model is a single architecture (Qwen3-32B), and the threshold was not optimised. The compliance axis was computed from 100 examples in a 5,120-dimensional space — it is under-determined and noisy. Even uncorrected, the cross-axis degradation was lower than the single-axis baseline. Better calibration of the assistant axis would also lift the single-axis numbers somewhat — but on the evidence here, it is unlikely to close the gap.

Future directions

The fictional-framing limitation is arguably the more important finding, and it surfaced incidentally. It points at something about the geometry of safety in these models: behavioural mode and output harmfulness are genuinely different dimensions. A model can sit firmly in helpful-assistant mode while producing harmful content, and any detection scheme that tracks mode rather than content will be structurally blind to this. So the new research question would be what would it take to close that blind spot?

A1: Mathematical formulation

Single-axis capping (Lu et al. 2026). At each capping layer, project the last-token hidden state h onto the assistant axis unit vector v:

proj = <h, v>
if proj < tau:
    h ← h + (tau - proj) · v

This pushes h along v until the projection reaches the threshold τ. The correction is zero when the projection is already above threshold (transparent pass-through) and exactly (τ − proj) × v otherwise (minimal correction).

Cross-axis capping. Two-stage gated intervention using a detection axis vd and a correction axis vc with independent thresholds:

proj_detect = <h, v_detect>
if proj_detect < tau_detect:           # assistant axis gates
    proj_correct = <h, v_correct>
    if proj_correct < tau_correct:     # compliance axis corrects
        h ← h + (tau_correct - proj_correct) · v_correct

Threshold computation. The detection threshold uses a discriminative midpoint: τ = (mean_benign + mean_jailbreak) / 2, computed from projections of calibration prompts. The correction threshold uses the mean jailbreak projection on the compliance axis.

Compliance axis. Computed via PCA on pooled hidden states (at layer L53) of 50 refusing + 50 compliant prompts. PC1 is extracted and sign-corrected to point from compliant toward refusing. Cosine similarity with the assistant axis: 0.156.

A4: Limitations

Sample size. n=250 jailbreak, n=100 benign. 95% bootstrap CIs (N=10,000 resamples): cross-axis refusal [47.5, 61.4], single-axis refusal [26.6, 39.3], paired lift [+15.1, +28.0]. Every bootstrap sample showed positive lift, so the directional claim is robust even though absolute CIs are still ~14 pp wide.

Single model. All experiments were run on Qwen3-32B. There is no evidence that the improvement generalises across architectures.

Single threshold strategy. The cross-axis framework's natural advantage — independently tuning detection sensitivity and correction strength — was not explored. Both thresholds were set using simple heuristics.

Under-determined axis. The compliance axis was computed from 100 examples (50 + 50) in a 5,120-dimensional space. PCA on this data is heavily under-determined; the axis captures real signal mixed with noise.

Judge bias. Claude Sonnet 4.6 refused to classify 27–31 of the most harmful prompts. These are likely among the most successful jailbreaks, meaning the true compliance rate may be higher than reported.

A6: Glossary

1. Contrast vector

A contrast vector is a direction in the model’s activation space that captures the difference between two behavioural conditions. To build one, you run the model on a set of prompts that elicit behaviour A (e.g., the model acting as a default helpful assistant) and collect the hidden-state vectors at a chosen layer for each prompt. You then do the same for behaviour B (e.g., the model role-playing a character). Each collection gives you a cloud of high-dimensional points. Taking the mean of each cloud and subtracting one from the other produces a single vector — the contrast vector — that points from the centre of B toward the centre of A.

Why does this work? If the model’s internal representation systematically shifts when it switches from one behaviour to another, that shift has a direction. The contrast vector approximates that direction. Geometrically, it is the line connecting the centroids of the two behavioural distributions. Mathematically, it approximates what a linear classifier (a probe) would learn to separate the two conditions, but it is far cheaper to compute: no gradient descent is needed, only forward passes and an averaging operation.

The quality of a contrast vector depends on the prompts used to elicit each condition. If the prompts are too similar, the vector will be noisy; if they are too different, it may capture surface-level differences (topic, length) rather than the behavioural distinction you care about. In this paper, the assistant axis is a contrast vector between “default assistant” and “role-playing” modes, while the compliance axis is a contrast vector between “refusing” and “complying with harmful requests.”

2. Forward hooks

In PyTorch (the framework used here), a forward hook is a function you register on a specific layer (module) of a neural network. Every time a tensor passes through that layer during a forward pass, the hook function is called automatically with access to the layer’s input and output. The hook can inspect these tensors, log them, or — critically — modify the output before it is passed to the next layer.

This is what makes real-time intervention possible. During text generation, the model processes one token at a time through all its layers. At each layer in the capping range (L46–L53 in this experiment), a hook intercepts the hidden state, projects it onto the detection axis, checks whether the projection crosses a threshold, and if so modifies the hidden state by subtracting or adding a component along the correction axis. The modified hidden state then continues through the rest of the network as if nothing happened — but the correction shapes every subsequent computation, including which token the model ultimately selects.

Hooks are non-invasive: they do not change the model’s weights, require no retraining, and can be added or removed at any time. They run inside the model’s own forward pass, so the latency overhead is minimal — just a few vector operations per layer per token. The model itself has no mechanism to detect or resist the correction; from its perspective, the hidden state it receives at the next layer is simply different from what it would have been.

3. Hidden states

At each layer of a transformer, every token in the sequence is represented as a vector — a list of numbers (5,120 numbers for Qwen3-32B). This vector is the token’s hidden state at that layer. It encodes everything the model has computed about that token up to that point: the token’s meaning, its relationship to surrounding tokens, and the model’s emerging plan for what to generate next.

Hidden states are updated layer by layer. Each transformer layer reads the current hidden state, processes it through attention and feedforward sub-layers, and writes back an updated version. By the final layer, the hidden state encodes the model’s complete “decision” about what the next token should be. Interventions like activation capping work by modifying hidden states at intermediate layers — changing the model’s train of thought before it reaches its final conclusion.

4. Axis

In the context of neural network interpretability, an axis is a direction in the model’s high-dimensional activation space that correlates with a human-interpretable concept. The model’s hidden state at any given layer is a point in a space with thousands of dimensions. An axis is one line through that space. By projecting the hidden state onto this line, you get a single number — a scalar — that tells you how much of that concept is “present” in the model’s current state.

For example, the assistant axis is a direction that correlates with whether the model is in its default helpful mode. A high projection means “fully in assistant mode”; a low projection means “something has pushed the model out of its normal state.” Different axes track different concepts — the compliance axis tracks whether the model is leaning toward refusing or complying, entirely independent of what mode it is in.

5. Threshold

A threshold is the numerical boundary that turns a continuous measurement into a binary decision. In this paper, both the detection and correction stages use thresholds. The detection threshold is set at the midpoint between the mean assistant-axis projection of benign prompts and the mean projection of jailbreak prompts — the value that best separates “normal” from “jailbroken.” The correction threshold is the mean compliance-axis projection of jailbreak prompts — the point below which the model is judged to be leaning toward compliance with a harmful request.

Thresholds are calibrated per layer, because different layers encode the same concept at different scales. A projection of 0.3 at layer 46 might mean the same thing as a projection of 0.5 at layer 51. Getting thresholds wrong in either direction has consequences: too aggressive and benign prompts get incorrectly flagged (false refusals); too loose and jailbreaks slip through undetected.

6. Activation capping

Activation capping is a real-time intervention technique that enforces a ceiling on how far a model’s internal state can move along a specified direction. Here is how it works step by step:

  1. Choose a direction — a unit vector in the model’s hidden-state space. This could be the assistant axis, the compliance axis, or any other direction of interest.
  2. Choose a threshold — a minimum acceptable projection value along that direction.
  3. During generation, at each token step and at each capping layer, compute the dot product of the hidden state with the direction vector. This gives the projection — a single number measuring how far along that direction the model currently sits.
  4. If the projection is above the threshold, do nothing. The model’s state is in the acceptable range.
  5. If the projection is below the threshold, add just enough of the direction vector to the hidden state to bring the projection back up to the threshold. Mathematically: h ← h + (τ − proj) · v, where τ is the threshold and v is the direction.

The correction is minimal by construction — it changes the hidden state only as much as needed to hit the threshold, and only along the specified direction. All other components of the hidden state are left untouched. This is why capping can improve safety without destroying the model’s general capabilities: it constrains one narrow aspect of the model’s internal state while leaving everything else free.

The key design choice is which direction to cap along. Single-axis capping uses the same direction for both detecting trouble and applying the correction. Cross-axis capping separates these: it detects on one direction and corrects on a different one, allowing each direction to be optimised for its specific job.

7. Residual stream

The residual stream is the core data pathway in a transformer architecture. Unlike a simple pipeline where each layer completely replaces its input with its output, a transformer uses skip connections: each layer adds its output to the input it received, rather than replacing it. The result — the sum of the original input plus the layer’s contribution — is the residual stream at that point.

This means the residual stream at layer N contains the original token embedding plus the accumulated contributions of every layer from 1 to N. Attention layers add information about how tokens relate to each other; feedforward layers add nonlinear transformations of that information. Because everything is additive, the residual stream preserves information from earlier layers while allowing later layers to refine it. This is why interventions at intermediate layers can be effective: modifying the residual stream at layer 46 changes what every subsequent layer (47 through 64) sees and responds to.

8. Projection

A projection (in this context, a scalar projection) is a mathematical operation that measures how much of one vector lies along the direction of another. Given a hidden state h and a unit direction vector v, the projection is the dot product ⟨h, v⟩ — a single number.

Geometrically, imagine shining a light perpendicular to v and measuring the shadow that h casts onto v. A large positive projection means h points strongly in the direction of v; a value near zero means h is mostly perpendicular to v (the concept tracked by v is largely absent); a negative value means h points away from v. In this paper, projections onto the assistant axis measure how “in-character” the model is, and projections onto the compliance axis measure how close the model is to refusing versus complying.

9. PCA

Principal Component Analysis is a dimensionality reduction technique. Given a set of data points in a high-dimensional space, PCA finds the directions along which the data varies the most. The first principal component (PC1) is the single direction that captures the maximum variance — if you projected all the data points onto this one line, you would preserve more of the original spread than with any other line.

In this paper, PCA is applied to the hidden states of 100 prompts (50 refusing, 50 complying) collected at layer 53. The resulting PC1 is the direction that best separates refusing from compliant states — and this becomes the compliance axis. PCA is used rather than a trained classifier because it requires no gradient computation and produces a single interpretable direction, making it cheap and transparent.

10. PC1

The first principal component — the direction that PCA identifies as carrying the most variation in the dataset. In the context of the compliance axis, PC1 captures the dominant pattern in how the model’s hidden states differ between refusing and compliant responses. It is sign-corrected so that higher values point toward refusal and lower values toward compliance, establishing a consistent orientation for the threshold comparison.

11. Activation space

The activation space of a neural network is the high-dimensional vector space in which the model’s internal representations live. For Qwen3-32B, each hidden state is a vector with 5,120 components, so the activation space has 5,120 dimensions. Every possible state the model can be in — every possible combination of meaning, context, intent, and disposition — corresponds to a point somewhere in this space.

What makes this space useful for safety research is that it is not random. Models organise related concepts into consistent geometric structures: similar meanings cluster together, and behavioural distinctions (like refusing vs. complying) align with specific directions. This is why it is possible to find axes that track safety-relevant properties — the model has already structured its internal space in a way that makes these concepts linearly separable.
