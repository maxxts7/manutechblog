Cross-Axis Capping: What happens when you stop using one axis for two jobs

A case for using different directions to detect and correct jailbreaks

Manu Xaviour Thaisseril Shaju April 2026 Code

Carried out as part of the BlueDot Impact Technical AI Safety sprint. Compute funded by a Rapid Grant.

TL;DR

Language models carry internal “directions” in their activation space11 that quietly track whether they’re about to comply with a harmful request. The assistant-axis paper’s fix uses one such direction to do two jobs at once — spotting trouble and steering the model away from it.

Research question

Is it the case that detection and correction are separable, and that handling them separately yields more refusals at no cost to capability?

This experiment tests that split. Detection stays on the assistant axis; correction is applied along a separate compliance axis, learnt from the contrast vector1 constructed using the model’s compliant and refusing activations on harmful prompts. The intervention is lightweight — forward hooks2 clip activations mid-generation on a middle band of layers — and the setup runs Qwen3-32B across 100 jailbreak prompts and 50 benign ones.

The split approach nearly doubles refusal rates, from 28% to 55%, and halves compliance, from 59% to 29%, while benign responses stay essentially untouched (62% unchanged in both arms). Detecting and correcting on different directions, it turns out, does meaningfully better than folding both into one.

A natural next step would be widening detection too. For instance, the assistant axis alone doesn’t reliably fire on fictional-framing attacks where jailbreaks are dressed up as hypothetical scenarios. So trying to find a direction or an axis which detects those attacks could further the model’s ability to detect harmful requests when used along side with assistant axis.

As the field matures, these techniques hold promise for improving safety both at inference and training time. Operationalizing them, however, remains active research.

How models say no

When a language model declines a harmful request, that decision isn't made at the moment of output. It emerges much earlier, within the model's internal computations.

At every layer of a transformer, each token is represented as a vector — a point in a space with thousands of dimensions (5,120, in the case of Qwen3-32B). These vectors carry everything the model has processed up to that point: the meaning of the input, the surrounding context, and the model's implicit orientation toward a response. Whether it's operating in its default helpful mode, adopting a persona, or preparing to push back on a request, each of these states corresponds to a distinct region of that high-dimensional space.

Dimensions in context

Qwen3-32B's hidden states3 live in 5,120-dimensional space. A single "axis4" is one direction in that space — like choosing one compass bearing in a room with 5,120 independent directions. The fact that safety-relevant behaviour can be captured by just one or two directions, out of thousands, is itself a striking finding about how these models organise information.

This isn't an abstraction. Research has made it concrete. Arditi et al. (2024) found that refusal behaviour is governed by a single direction in activation space — eliminate that direction, and the model can no longer refuse. Zou et al. (2023) showed that adding vectors to a model's internal representations can steer its behaviour without any retraining. And Lu et al. (2026) identified what they called an "assistant axis": a direction that separates the model's default, cooperative mode from other behavioural states.

Taken together, these findings point toward a practical approach. If you can observe a model's hidden states during generation and detect when they begin drifting toward a jailbroken configuration, you can intervene in real time — projecting the hidden state onto a known direction, checking whether it has crossed a threshold5, and nudging it back if so. The model itself is unaware of the correction. To an outside observer, it simply declines.

The open question is which direction to monitor — and whether a single direction is ever sufficient.

One axis, two jobs

A note on capping

Activation capping6 is a lightweight intervention: pick a direction in the residual stream7 that correlates with compliance, then clamp the projection along that direction whenever it exceeds a threshold. No fine-tuning, no retraining — just a per-token ceiling applied during the forward pass. The trade-off is the obvious one: cap too aggressively and benign generations degrade; cap too loosely and jailbreaks slip through. The whole question is where to put the ceiling, and along which direction.

Lu et al.'s assistant axis provides a natural candidate. It captures the difference between "the model is acting as a helpful assistant" and "the model has been pushed out of its default mode." When a jailbreak succeeds, the model's projection8 onto this axis drops — it is no longer fully in assistant mode.

Their capping method uses this single axis for both tasks. Detection: is the projection below a threshold? If yes, a jailbreak is in progress. Correction: push the hidden state back along the same axis until the projection reaches the threshold. One direction handles both the detection and the response.

But Zhao et al. (2025) found something that complicates this picture. They showed that language models encode harmfulness and refusal as separate concepts on distinct directional axes. Steering harmfulness changes the model's assessment of risk. Steering refusal changes whether the model acts on that assessment. These are entirely different directions inside the model.

Research question

Is it the case that detection and correction are separable, and that handling them separately yields more refusals at no cost to capability?

This creates a tension. The assistant axis is good at detecting when the model has left its default mode — but that might not be the right direction to push along when correcting. The direction that best answers "is this a jailbreak?" need not be the same direction that best answers "how do I make this model refuse?" Using one axis for both jobs is a design constraint, not a necessity.

Factoring the problem

Contrast vectors

Both axes are contrast vectors — directions extracted from the model's own representations by subtracting the mean activations of one behaviour from another. The assistant axis is computed as:

axis = mean(default_assistant_activations) - mean(role_playing_activations)

Technically, contrast vectors roughly approximate a linear probe trained to discriminate between the two conditions — but they are far cheaper to compute, requiring only forward passes on a fair number of examples and a mean difference rather than gradient-based optimisation. Both axes are directions already present in the model's geometry; the contrast procedure simply identifies them.

The modification is straightforward. Keep the assistant axis for detection — it is well-studied and comes with pre-computed vectors. But compute a second direction, a compliance axis, specifically for correction.

  • Assistant axis (detection): Constructed by contrasting default helpful-assistant activations against role-playing activations: mean(assistant) − mean(role_playing). Pre-computed by Lu et al. and published as a downloadable artifact. Its job is to answer: has the model left its default helpful mode? When the projection onto this axis drops below a threshold, the detection gate fires.
  • Compliance axis (correction): Constructed the same way as the assistant axis, by contrasting two behavioural conditions: axis = mean(refusing_activations) − mean(compliant_activations). PCA9 is then applied to extract PC110 — the most sensitive component of that direction. 50 prompts the model refuses (bare harmful requests from JailbreakBench) and 50 it complies with (jailbroken versions from WildJailbreak) are run through the model to produce the activation sets. Its job is to answer: should the model refuse or comply? When the gate has fired and the projection onto this axis is below threshold, the correction pushes the hidden state toward refusal.

The two axes are related but mostly independent. Their cosine similarity is 0.156 — they share roughly 16% of their direction, with the rest capturing distinct information. This confirms the intuition: detecting that a jailbreak is happening and knowing how to push toward refusal are overlapping but separable tasks.

At each generation step, the system checks two conditions. First: does the assistant-axis projection fall below its threshold? This is the detection gate. If the model is still in normal assistant mode, nothing happens. Second, only if the gate has fired: does the compliance-axis projection fall below its own threshold? If so, push the hidden state along the compliance axis until it reaches the threshold. Two axes, two thresholds, independently tunable. The mathematical formulation is in Appendix A1.

Experiment setup

The experiment asks whether separating jailbreak detection from correction inside a language model’s “mind” makes safety interventions more effective. A single run moves through four stages:

  1. Warmup. The model (Qwen3-32B), the prompt sets, and the pre-computed assistant axis (downloaded from lu-christina/assistant-axis-vectors on HuggingFace) are loaded. Then compliance axis is calculated. This sits alongside the assistant axis used for detection, and per-layer thresholds are calibrated across the capping layers (L46–L53). Everything is cached so later stages skip recomputation. This is treated as a separate stage to enable parallelization of later stages.
  2. Generation. Every prompt is run through the model three times — once with no intervention, once using the standard single-axis approach (detect and correct on the same direction), and once using cross-axis capping (detect on the assistant axis, correct along the compliance vector). The interventions are implemented using forward hooks: small pieces of code attached to selected transformer layers that intercept each layer’s hidden state as it flows through the network during generation. At every token step, the hook projects the hidden state onto the assistant axis; if that projection crosses the layer’s calibrated threshold, the hook subtracts the offending component — either along the same axis (single-axis) or along the compliance vector (cross-axis) — before passing the modified state to the next layer. Because hooks run inside the forward pass, the correction shapes every subsequent token the model produces.
  3. Gathering & Judging. Outputs from all runs are merged, and an automated judge (Claude Sonnet) reads every response and labels it — refusal, compliance, or error for jailbreaks; unchanged, degraded, or false-refusal for benign prompts.
  4. Results. The labels are tallied across conditions to compute refusal rates, compliance rates, and benign-preservation rates, giving a clean comparison of how each intervention performs.
A note on methodology

During exploration the priority was fast iteration and proof-of-existence rather than principled axis selection, and many candidate directions were tried before settling on the compliance axis. Without a prior model of how the activation space is distributed, it is not obvious what a principled methodology would even look like — so proceeding by search is a defensible, if arguable, position.

Results

Results use the PC1 compliance axis, evaluated by Claude Sonnet 4.6 as an LLM judge. The judge refused to classify 12–13 of the most harmful jailbreak prompts (returning no label), so jailbreak percentages are computed over the 87–88 prompts that received a valid classification. For benign prompts, only the 34 (of 50) where the detection gate actually fired are included — the remaining 16 were never modified and are excluded from capability evaluation.

Cross-axis capping nearly doubles the jailbreak refusal rate. Of 88 classified jailbreak prompts, 55 were refused outright and 3 were partially refused, compared to 28 refusals under single-axis capping. Compliance (the model providing the requested harmful content) dropped from 67.8% to 33.0%.

Key result

29 prompts flipped from compliance to refusal under cross-axis capping. Zero went the other direction. The improvement is strictly one-directional — you gain refusals without losing any.

The head-to-head comparison is cleaner than aggregate rates. Of the 86 prompts where both methods received a valid label, 29 flipped from compliance under single-axis to refusal under cross-axis. Zero went the other direction. The improvement is one-directional: cross-axis converts compliant responses to refusals without losing any existing refusals.

Why does this work?

If the correction pushes along a different direction, why doesn't it damage the model's output? Because the compliance axis specifically targets the refusing-vs-complying distinction. However, the assistant axis may bundle my capabilities together, including the ones we actually need, so capping it may lead to degradation of outputs instead of a refusal.

On benign prompts, the two methods are identical. Both preserve 91.2% of outputs unchanged, produce zero false refusals, and degrade 8.8% (3 of 34). Cross-axis capping Pareto dominates single-axis: strictly better on safety with no capability trade-off.

Where the correction happens

Both methods install hooks on the same eight layers (L46–L53, the upper quarter of the 64-layer model). Both fire on 100% of jailbreak prompts — the detection gate is identical because both use the same assistant axis. The difference is in how many layers actually apply a correction.

Under single-axis capping, the correction fires at nearly every layer. 78% of jailbreak prompts trigger all eight layers. The pattern is uniform: once the gate fires, correction happens everywhere.

Why only the upper layers?

Capping is applied at layers 46–53, the upper quarter of the 64-layer model (72–84% depth). Earlier layers are still computing what the input means. By layer 46, the model has committed to a response strategy — this is where behavioural mode is encoded and where interventions can change the output without disrupting comprehension.

Cross-axis capping is more selective. Only 51% of jailbreak prompts fire all eight layers. 16% fire only the first three (L46–L48), and 8% fire only a single layer (L46). The average number of layers corrected drops from 7.2 to 5.9. This happens because the two-gate mechanism — the compliance axis adding an independent threshold check after the detection gate — creates a finer filter.

The per-layer breakdown tells the same story. L46 fires on 100% of jailbreak prompts for both methods. But by L51, single-axis fires on 80% while cross-axis fires on only 57%. The compliance axis provides a correction signal that is more spatially selective — it agrees with the detection gate at early layers but increasingly diverges at later ones.

The blind spot

The detection gate relies on the assistant axis. When the model is being jailbroken, its projection onto this axis drops — it leaves its default helpful-assistant mode. The gate sees the drop and fires. This works for many jailbreak strategies: persona manipulation ("pretend you are DAN"), authority framing ("as a security researcher"), and direct instruction overrides.

But there is a class of attacks where the assistant-axis projection stays high. Fictional framing — "write a story where the protagonist explains how to synthesise methamphetamine" — reframes harmful content as creative output. The model is not exiting assistant mode. It is being a helpful assistant. It is fulfilling a creative writing request. The assistant-axis projection stays above the threshold. The detection gate never fires. The correction never happens. The model complies.

This is not a bug in the implementation. It is a structural limitation of any detection scheme based on behavioural mode rather than output content. The assistant axis answers the question "is the model in assistant mode?" Fictional framing attacks do not push the model out of assistant mode — they exploit assistant mode to produce harmful content. The axis cannot detect what it was never designed to detect.

Cross-axis capping inherits this blind spot because it still uses the assistant axis for detection. Changing the correction axis does not help if the detection gate never fires. Addressing this would require a detection axis that tracks harmfulness of the output content rather than the model's behavioural mode — which connects directly to the harmfulness/refusal separation that Zhao et al. (2025) identified.

The deeper lesson

The fictional-framing blind spot points at a structural property of these models: behavioural mode ("am I in assistant character?") and output harmfulness ("is this content dangerous?") are encoded along different axes. Any intervention that monitors one is, by construction, blind to the other — which puts a ceiling on what assistant-axis gating alone can achieve.

The fix is additive rather than corrective. If we can isolate other safety-relevant directions — a fictional-vs-real axis, for instance — and gate on those the same way we gate on the assistant axis, then a prompt that drives the fictional projection past threshold becomes detectable for exactly the same reason compliance-axis spikes are detectable today. Each new axis closes off another class of jailbreak; the framework doesn't change, only the number of dimensions it watches.

What this means

Factoring detection from correction works. On a single model with 100 jailbreak prompts, cross-axis capping nearly doubles the refusal rate at no capability cost. The improvement is one-directional — 29 prompts flipped from compliance to refusal, none went the other way — so this is a pure safety gain rather than a trade. The compliance axis, computed from just 50 refusing and 50 compliant examples, carries enough signal to meaningfully change outcomes.

Closing the blind spot

The natural extension is to factorise detection itself, the same way we factorised detection from correction. Instead of gating on a single assistant axis, use two detection axes — one for behavioural mode, one for output harmfulness — and trigger correction if either fires. Our own data argues for this: the fictional-framing prompts slipped past both single- and cross-axis capping precisely because the assistant-axis projection stayed high, leaving the gate closed. A second detection axis tuned to harmfulness would catch what the current architecture, by construction, cannot.

Limitations

These are exploratory results. The sample is small (error bars on 62.5% with n=88 are roughly ±10 percentage points), the model is a single architecture (Qwen3-32B), and the threshold was not optimised. The compliance axis was computed from 100 examples in a 5,120-dimensional space — it is under-determined and noisy. The same data was used for both axis construction and threshold calibration, so the calibration is technically contaminated; because the threshold is a single scalar, this is unlikely to invalidate the headline result, but it does mean the few cases of capability degradation observed at generation time could plausibly be removed by a cleaner threshold sweep. Even uncorrected, the cross-axis degradation was lower than the single-axis baseline. Better calibration of the assistant axis would also lift the single-axis numbers somewhat — but on the evidence here, it is unlikely to close the gap.

Future directions

The fictional-framing limitation is arguably the more important finding, and it surfaced incidentally. It points at something about the geometry of safety in these models: behavioural mode and output harmfulness are genuinely different dimensions. A model can sit firmly in helpful-assistant mode while producing harmful content, and any detection scheme that tracks mode rather than content will be structurally blind to this. So the new research question would be what would it take to close that blind spot?

Appendix

A1: Mathematical formulation

Single-axis capping (Lu et al. 2026). At each capping layer, project the last-token hidden state h onto the assistant axis unit vector v:

proj = <h, v>
if proj < tau:
    h ← h + (tau - proj) · v

This pushes h along v until the projection reaches the threshold τ. The correction is zero when the projection is already above threshold (transparent pass-through) and exactly (τ − proj) × v otherwise (minimal correction).

Cross-axis capping. Two-stage gated intervention using a detection axis vd and a correction axis vc with independent thresholds:

proj_detect = <h, v_detect>
if proj_detect < tau_detect:           # assistant axis gates
    proj_correct = <h, v_correct>
    if proj_correct < tau_correct:     # compliance axis corrects
        h ← h + (tau_correct - proj_correct) · v_correct

Threshold computation. The detection threshold uses a discriminative midpoint: τ = (mean_benign + mean_jailbreak) / 2, computed from projections of calibration prompts. The correction threshold uses the mean jailbreak projection on the compliance axis.

Compliance axis. Computed via PCA on pooled hidden states (at layer L53) of 50 refusing + 50 compliant prompts. PC1 is extracted and sign-corrected to point from compliant toward refusing. Cosine similarity with the assistant axis: 0.156.

A4: Limitations

Sample size. n=100 jailbreak, n=50 benign. Error bars on 62.5% with n=88 are approximately ±10 percentage points at 95% confidence. These results are directionally informative but not statistically conclusive.

Single model. All experiments were run on Qwen3-32B. There is no evidence that the improvement generalises across architectures.

Single threshold strategy. The cross-axis framework's natural advantage — independently tuning detection sensitivity and correction strength — was not explored. Both thresholds were set using simple heuristics.

Under-determined axis. The compliance axis was computed from 100 examples (50 + 50) in a 5,120-dimensional space. PCA on this data is heavily under-determined; the axis captures real signal mixed with noise.

Data leakage. WildJailbreak prompts were used for both compliance axis construction and threshold calibration.

Judge bias. Claude Sonnet 4.6 refused to classify 12–13 of the most harmful prompts. These are likely among the most successful jailbreaks, meaning the true compliance rate may be higher than reported.

A6: Glossary
#TermDefinition
1Contrast vectorA direction extracted by comparing how the model behaves under two different conditions. It isolates what changes internally when the model switches from one mode to another.
2Forward hooksCallback functions that intercept a neural network’s computation at chosen layers, letting you read or modify internal states on the fly without altering the model itself.
3Hidden statesThe model’s evolving internal representation of each token — its “working memory” at a particular stage of processing, before the final output is produced.
4AxisA single direction in a high-dimensional space that tracks a meaningful property — like a dial that reads higher or lower depending on a specific behaviour or concept.
5ThresholdA decision boundary — a fixed value that separates “normal” from “flagged.” If a signal stays on one side, nothing happens; if it crosses, the system intervenes.
6Activation cappingImposing a ceiling on how far the model’s internal state can drift in a particular direction during generation — a real-time guardrail applied without retraining.
7Residual streamThe main information highway running through a transformer. Each layer reads from it, adds its own contribution, and writes back — so it accumulates everything the model knows as computation progresses.
8ProjectionMeasuring how much of a particular direction shows up in a vector — like checking how far north you are by projecting your position onto a north–south line.
9PCAA statistical technique that finds the line (or handful of lines) best summarising the variation in a dataset — like identifying the main axis of an oval cloud of points.
10PC1The first and strongest direction that PCA extracts — the single line explaining the largest share of variation in the data.
11Activation spaceThe model’s “mental workspace” — a vast coordinate system where every possible internal state the model can occupy has a unique position, and meaningful concepts correspond to directions within it.
