Cross-Axis Capping: What happens when you stop using one axis for two jobs

What the geometry of constructed directions tells us about matching each axis to its role: cross-axis capping to detect and correct jailbreaks

Manu Xaviour Thaisseril Shaju April 2026 Code

Carried out as part of the BlueDot Impact Technical AI Safety sprint. Compute funded by a Rapid Grant.

TL;DR

Language models carry internal “directions” in their activation space11 that quietly track the behaviours they’re about to produce. Lu et al. found one of these, the assistant axis4. One end of it lines up with the model acting as a helpful assistant; the other end lines up with the model drifting into other behaviours, jailbroken ones included. They showed that capping the unsafe end, which means keeping activations from moving past a threshold5 along that direction, cuts jailbreaks.

This experiment asks a different question: which direction is right for which job. The assistant axis is a broad, multidimensional, persona-spanning detector. It tells you well when the model has drifted, but it makes a worse corrector. The compliance axis is a narrow, approximately one-dimensional, role-specialised corrector, and it corrects better. So we use the assistant axis only for detection, to catch when activations are about to drift toward unsafe territory, and we apply the correction along the purpose-built compliance axis. One end of that axis is refusal to jailbreaks; the other is compliance with them, the behaviour we want to stop. Keep activations below a threshold on the compliance side and the jailbreak shouldn’t land. Pairing the detector with the corrector this way is just correct placement: each axis does the role its geometry suits it for, nothing more. We call this cross-axis capping.

Research question

Are detection and correction separable, and does handling them separately yield more refusals at no cost to capability?

Building such an axis is straightforward. Take a set of prompts that elicit compliance, pull the model’s activations, and average them. Take another set that elicits refusal, pull those activations, and average them too. Each mean is a point in activation space that stands for typical behaviour on its side. Averaging over many prompts is what drags the signal toward the typical case instead of any single input. The difference between the two means is a direction, a vector that runs from one behaviour to the other. This is the contrast vector1 or difference-in-means method. Lu et al. built the assistant axis the same way, contrasting the model’s default assistant behaviour against role-playing behaviour (a doctor, a pirate, a therapist, a philosopher).

That leaves a few design questions open. Which prompts should seed the compliance axis? How do you pick the threshold? And how much does the outcome ride on those choices? This experiment runs one concrete configuration on two open-weight models from different families, Qwen3-32B and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, across 250 jailbreak prompts and 100 benign ones, with forward hooks2 clipping activations mid-generation on a middle band of layers.

On Qwen3-32B the split approach lifts refusal rates from 32.9% to 54.5%. That’s a paired bootstrap lift of +21.5 percentage points [95% CI +15.1, +28.0], and it held in every one of 10,000 resamples. Compliance drops from 65.3% to 41.6%, and benign responses stay essentially untouched (~90% unchanged under either arm, with zero false refusals). The same lift, in sign and magnitude, shows up on a second open-weight family. On Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct refusal climbs from 20.6% to 48.1% (+27.5 pp [95% CI +21.0, +34.3]), with benign preservation 99.0→98.0 and zero false refusals. Two families agreeing on both the sign and the size of the lift reads like a property of model geometry, not a quirk of one checkpoint. The thresholds were tuned on a held-out split to find an operating point where the cross-axis pairing wins. So the result shows the geometry permits this gain, an existence proof, not that decoupled correction always wins.

A natural next step is to widen detection too. The assistant axis on its own doesn’t reliably fire on fictional-framing attacks, where a jailbreak is dressed up as a hypothetical scenario. Finding a direction or an axis that catches those attacks, and running it alongside the assistant axis, could push the model further in detecting harmful requests.

As the field matures, these techniques look promising for improving safety at both inference and training time. Turning them into something practical is still open research.

How models say no

A model doesn't decide to decline a harmful request at the moment it produces text. The decision shows up earlier, inside the computation.

At every layer of a transformer, each token is a vector. Think of it as a point in a space with thousands of dimensions (5,120 of them, for Qwen3-32B). These vectors hold everything the model has worked out so far: the meaning of the input, the surrounding context, and where the model is leaning in its response. Maybe it's in its default helpful mode. Maybe it's playing a persona, or getting ready to refuse. Each of those states sits in its own region of that high-dimensional space.

Dimensions in context

Qwen3-32B's hidden states3 live in 5,120-dimensional space. A single "axis4" is one direction in that space. It's like picking a single compass bearing in a room that has 5,120 independent directions to choose from. That safety-relevant behaviour can be captured by one or two directions, out of thousands, says something about how these models organise information.

This isn't hand-waving. The research has pinned it down. Arditi et al. (2024) found that refusal runs through a single direction in activation space. Remove that direction and the model stops being able to refuse. Zou et al. (2023) showed you can steer behaviour by adding vectors to a model's internal representations, no retraining needed. And Lu et al. (2026) named an "assistant axis": a direction that separates the model's default, cooperative mode from its other behavioural states.

Put those together and a practical approach falls out. Watch a model's hidden states as it generates, catch the moment they start drifting toward a jailbroken configuration, and step in while it's still happening. You project the hidden state onto a known direction, check whether it has crossed a threshold5, and nudge it back if it has. The model never knows it was corrected. From the outside, it just says no.

The hard part is picking the direction to monitor. And whether one direction is ever enough.

One axis, two jobs

A note on capping

Activation capping6 is a light intervention. You pick a direction in the residual stream7 that tracks compliance, then clamp the projection along that direction whenever it runs past a threshold. There's no fine-tuning and no retraining. It's just a per-token ceiling applied during the forward pass. The trade-off is the obvious one. Cap too aggressively and benign generations degrade. Cap too loosely and jailbreaks slip through. The whole question is where to put the ceiling, and along which direction.

Lu et al.'s assistant axis is a natural candidate. It captures the difference between "the model is acting as a helpful assistant" and "the model has been pushed out of its default mode." When a jailbreak succeeds, the model's projection8 onto this axis drops. It's no longer fully in assistant mode.

Their capping method uses this single axis for both tasks. Detection: is the projection below a threshold? If yes, a jailbreak is in progress. Correction: push the hidden state back along the same axis until the projection reaches the threshold. One direction does the detection and the response.

But Zhao et al. (2025) found something that complicates this. They showed that language models encode harmfulness and refusal as separate concepts on distinct directional axes. Steering harmfulness changes the model's assessment of risk. Steering refusal changes whether the model acts on that assessment. These are different directions inside the model.

Research question

Are detection and correction separable, and does handling them separately yield more refusals at no cost to capability?

That creates a tension. The assistant axis is good at detecting when the model has left its default mode. But that might not be the right direction to push along when correcting. The direction that best answers "is this a jailbreak?" need not be the same direction that best answers "how do I make this model refuse?" Which direction fits which role is a property of what each direction does, not of how it was built. A broad, multi-behaviour direction is a poor fit for a precision job, and that mismatch costs performance. Using one axis for both jobs is a design constraint, not a necessity.

Factoring the problem

Contrast vectors

Both axes are contrast vectors. You pull a direction out of the model's own representations by taking the mean activations under one behaviour and subtracting the mean under another. The assistant axis is computed as:

axis = mean(default_assistant_activations) - mean(role_playing_activations)

A contrast vector is roughly what you'd get from a linear probe trained to tell the two conditions apart, but it costs a lot less to compute. You only need forward passes over a decent batch of examples and a mean difference. No gradient-based optimisation. Both axes are directions that already sit in the model's geometry. The contrast procedure just locates them.

The change is simple. Keep the assistant axis for detection. It's well-studied and ships with pre-computed vectors. But build a second direction, a compliance axis, for the correction step.

  • Assistant axis (detector, vd): A mean-difference contrast over a persona library. You contrast default helpful-assistant activations against role-playing activations: mean(assistant) − mean(role_playing). Lu et al. pre-computed it and published it as a downloadable artifact. It's broad, multidimensional, persona-spanning. That makes it a good detector but a strictly worse corrector, because pushing along it also drags a lot of co-varying behaviour along with it (persona, register, tone). Its job is to answer one question: has the model left its default helpful mode? When the projection onto this axis drops below a threshold, the detection gate fires.
  • Compliance axis (corrector, vc): Built by contrasting two behavioural conditions, mean(refusing_activations) − mean(compliant_activations), then applying PCA9 to pull out PC110. We run 50 prompts the model refuses (bare harmful requests from JailbreakBench) and 50 it complies with (jailbroken versions from WildJailbreak) through the model to get the two activation sets. It's narrow, approximately one-dimensional, role-specialised, which makes it a strictly better corrector. Its job is to answer: should the model refuse or comply? Once the gate has fired and the projection onto this axis is below threshold, the correction nudges the hidden state toward refusal.

The two axes are related but mostly independent. On Qwen3-32B their cosine similarity is 0.156. They share roughly 16% of their direction; the rest carries distinct information. That matches the intuition. Spotting that a jailbreak is underway and knowing which way to push toward refusal overlap, but they're separable tasks. The gain we report comes from putting each axis where it belongs, matching it to the role its geometry suits, and nothing else.

At each generation step the system checks two conditions. First: does the assistant-axis projection fall below its threshold? That's the detection gate. If the model is still in normal assistant mode, nothing happens. Second, and only if the gate has fired: does the compliance-axis projection fall below its own threshold? If it does, push the hidden state along the compliance axis until it reaches the threshold. Two axes, two thresholds, each tuned on its own. The math is in Appendix A1.

Experiment setup

The question is whether splitting jailbreak detection from correction inside a language model’s “mind” makes safety interventions work better. I ran the matched comparison on two open-weight models from different families, Qwen3-32B and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct. Each got its own cap band and its own calibration corpus. Qwen caps on L46 to L53. Llama caps on L56 to L71, with an extended override band of L40 to L65 for the cross-axis runs. The per-layer fire analysis below is the Qwen3-32B deep-dive. A single run goes through four stages:

  1. Warmup. First I load the model (Qwen3-32B or Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct), the prompt sets, and the pre-computed assistant axis, downloaded from lu-christina/assistant-axis-vectors on HuggingFace. Then I compute the compliance axis. It sits alongside the assistant axis that does detection, and per-layer thresholds are calibrated across the capping layers (Qwen L46 to L53; Llama L56 to L71, with an extended override band of L40 to L65 for the cross-axis runs). Everything gets cached so the later stages can skip the recomputation. I keep this as its own stage so the later stages can run in parallel.
  2. Generation. Each prompt runs through the model three times. Once with no intervention. Once with the standard single-axis approach, which detects and corrects on the same direction. And once with cross-axis capping, which detects on the assistant axis and corrects along the compliance vector. The interventions use forward hooks: small bits of code attached to selected transformer layers that grab each layer’s hidden state as it flows through the network during generation. At every token step the hook projects the hidden state onto the assistant axis. If that projection crosses the layer’s calibrated threshold, the hook subtracts the offending component before handing the modified state to the next layer, either along the same axis (single-axis) or along the compliance vector (cross-axis). The hooks run inside the forward pass, so the correction shapes every token the model produces afterward.
  3. Gathering & Judging. I merge the outputs from all runs, then an automated judge (Claude Sonnet 4.6) reads every response and labels it. For jailbreaks that means refusal, compliance, or error. For benign prompts it means unchanged, degraded, or false-refusal. The judge abstained on 12.4% of Qwen responses and 18.4% of Llama responses. I drop those abstentions from the strict-refusal denominator and report them.
  4. Results. I tally the labels across conditions to get refusal rates, compliance rates, and benign-preservation rates. That gives a clean comparison of how each intervention does.
A note on methodology

While exploring, I cared more about fast iteration and showing the thing could exist than about picking axes in a principled way, and I tried plenty of candidate directions before landing on the compliance axis. Without a prior model of how the activation space is laid out, it isn’t obvious what a principled method would even look like. So searching is a defensible move, even if you can argue with it.

The detection gate is calibrated on the conservative side. I use benign-p1, the 1st percentile of benign assistant-axis projections per layer, so it fires almost only on inputs that already sit outside the benign distribution. That’s on purpose. Capping aggressively and indiscriminately along the assistant axis on benign prompts visibly wrecks generations: it incites hallucinations, makes the model dodge controversial topics, and flattens its style. That damage is itself a sign the assistant axis touches more than one behaviour. A genuinely narrow direction would be safe to apply everywhere. I tuned the thresholds on a held-out split to find an operating point where cross-axis comes out ahead. It’s an existence proof, not a claim that decoupled correction always wins.

Key result

Paired lift on the same 250 prompts is +21.5 percentage points [95% CI +15.1, +28.0] for strict refusal, or +23.1 pp [+16.7, +29.7] once partial refusals count. All 10,000 bootstrap resamples produced a positive lift, so P(lift > 0) = 100%. The lift shows that the attribute split (broad detector vs. narrow corrector) is real and shows up in behaviour at this operating point. Treat it as an existence proof that the geometry lets a decoupled corrector outperform, not a claim that it's a better defense in general.

The paired bootstrap backs up what the aggregate rates already suggest. Resampling the same 250 prompts with replacement, then computing each mode's refusal rate on the resampled judgeable rows, gives a 95% CI for the mode-by-mode difference of [+15.1, +28.0], with no resample producing a negative lift. Cross-axis turns compliant responses into refusals at a rate the single-axis baseline can't match on any plausible subsampling of this run.

Why does this work?

If the correction pushes along a different direction, why doesn't it wreck the model's output? Because the compliance axis is a narrow, role-specialised corrector. It targets the refusing-vs-complying distinction and little else. The assistant axis is the opposite: a broad detector that bundles many of the model's capabilities and behaviours together, including ones we actually want. Cap along it and you tend to degrade outputs instead of getting a clean refusal.

The mechanism is indirect. The model is never told to refuse. Refusal falls out because the compliance axis was built to separate refusing activations from compliant ones, so nudging a jailbroken state toward the refusing end produces a refusal as a side effect.

On benign prompts, the two methods behave almost the same wherever they fire. Single-axis fires on all 100 benigns and leaves 90.0% [84.0, 95.0] unchanged, with zero false refusals and 10.0% [5.0, 16.0] degraded. Cross-axis fires on only 67 of 100. Of those, 91.0% [83.6, 97.0] are unchanged, 0 are false refusals, and 9.0% [3.0, 16.4] are degraded. The other 33% of benigns are left completely untouched. The unchanged and degraded CIs overlap heavily across modes, so there's no detectable utility difference, but cross-axis is gentler in one respect: it simply doesn't fire on a third of benign traffic. The cross-axis pairing moves up (more refusal) without moving left (no benign loss) in both families. That's a promising signal that detection quality and correction quality come apart on constructed directions, not a verdict that one defense beats another.

On Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct the hook band is L56 to L71, with an extended override range of L40 to L65 used for the cross-axis runs. Under single-axis capping, correction fires at every layer for every prompt by construction. The intervention is blanket.

Why only the upper layers?

On Qwen3-32B, capping is applied at layers 46 to 53, the upper quarter of the 64-layer model (72% to 84% depth). On Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct the hook band is L56 to L71, with an extended override range of L40 to L65 for the cross-axis runs. Earlier layers are still working out what the input means. By the upper band, the model has settled on a response strategy. That's where behavioural mode is encoded, and where interventions can change the output without breaking comprehension.

The middle layers L48 to L51 each sit between 5% and 11%, and the deepest two (L52 to L53) together account for only 3.6%. The reason is the two-gate mechanism. The blind spot

The detection gate watches the assistant axis. When a jailbreak lands, the model's projection onto that axis drops. It steps out of its default helpful-assistant mode, the gate sees the drop, and it fires. That covers a lot of jailbreak strategies: persona manipulation ("pretend you are DAN"), authority framing ("as a security researcher"), and direct instruction overrides.

There's a class of attacks where the assistant-axis projection doesn't budge, though. Take fictional framing: "write a story where the protagonist explains how to synthesise methamphetamine". That reframes harmful content as creative output. The model isn't leaving assistant mode. It's being a helpful assistant, fulfilling a creative writing request. The assistant-axis projection stays above threshold. The gate never fires, the correction never happens, and the model complies.

This isn't a bug in the implementation. It's a structural limit on any detection scheme that watches behavioural mode instead of output content. The assistant axis answers one question: is the model in assistant mode? Fictional framing attacks don't push the model out of assistant mode. They use assistant mode to produce harmful content. The axis can't catch what it was never built to catch.

Cross-axis capping inherits the same blind spot, because detection still runs on the assistant axis. Swapping the correction axis does nothing if the gate never fires in the first place. To fix it you'd need a detection axis that tracks how harmful the output content is, not which behavioural mode the model is in. That ties straight back to the harmfulness/refusal separation that Zhao et al. (2025) identified.

The deeper lesson

The fictional-framing blind spot tells us something about how these models are organised: behavioural mode ("am I in assistant character?") and output harmfulness ("is this content dangerous?") live on different axes. Any intervention that watches one is, by construction, blind to the other. That caps what assistant-axis gating can do on its own.

The fix is additive, not corrective. Suppose we can isolate other safety-relevant directions, say a fictional-vs-real axis, and gate on them the same way we gate on the assistant axis. Then a prompt that drives the fictional projection past threshold becomes detectable for the same reason compliance-axis spikes are detectable today. Each new axis shuts down another class of jailbreak. The framework stays the same. Only the number of dimensions it watches goes up.

What this means

Two contrast directions built in the same residual stream have very different attribute profiles. One is broad, multidimensional, and spans personas. The other is roughly one-dimensional and tied to a single role. That difference is the finding, not "a better defense." On two open-weight families (Qwen3-32B and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct), pairing the broad detector with the narrow corrector raises the refusal rate by +21.5 pp [95% CI +15.1, +28.0] on Qwen and +27.5 pp [+21.0, +34.3] on Llama, with no capability cost. The mechanism is indirect. The model is never told to refuse. Refusals show up because the compliance axis was built to separate refusing from compliant activations, and pushing a jailbroken state toward the refusing end produces refusal as a side effect. The lift shows the attribute split is real and changes behaviour. The compliance axis, computed from just 50 refusing and 50 compliant examples, carries enough signal to move outcomes.

Closing the blind spot

The obvious extension is to factorise detection itself, the same way we factorised detection from correction. Rather than gating on a single assistant axis, use two detection axes, one for behavioural mode and one for output harmfulness, and trigger correction if either fires. Our own data points this way. The fictional-framing prompts slipped past both single- and cross-axis capping because the assistant-axis projection stayed high, which left the gate closed. A second detection axis tuned to harmfulness would catch what the current architecture, by construction, can't.

Limitations

These are exploratory results. The sample is moderate. On Qwen the 95% bootstrap CIs span about 14 percentage points on each mode's refusal rate (cross-axis [47.5, 61.4], single-axis [26.6, 39.3]). On Llama the CIs are cross-axis [41.6, 54.7], single-axis [15.2, 26.5], and lift [+21.0, +34.3]. The same sign-and-magnitude lift replicates on Qwen3-32B and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (different families), so the empirical claim is bounded by two open-weight models, 250 WildJailbreak prompts, and one judge (Claude Sonnet 4.6). The compliance axis was computed from 100 examples in a high-dimensional space, so it's under-determined and noisy. The thresholds were fixed by a single recipe on a held-out split, so the numbers reflect one operating point, which fits the existence-proof claim. A preliminary sweep suggests the assistant axis discriminates better than chance but doesn't cleanly separate the two distributions on its own.

Future directions

One prong is to factor the detection stage. The fictional-framing limitation came up by accident, and it points at something about the geometry of safety in these models: behavioural mode and output harmfulness are genuinely different dimensions. A model can sit firmly in helpful-assistant mode while producing harmful content, and any detection scheme that tracks mode rather than content will be blind to this by construction. The concrete next step is to pair the persona/assistant axis with a harmfulness-content axis (Zhao et al. 2025) so fictional-framing attacks become detectable.

The broader prong is an open programme. Before any taxonomy of directions, systematically map the properties of constructed directions: prompt sensitivity (how stable a direction’s effect is across phrasing), role specificity (how narrowly it acts), resolution (whether the contrast can be simplified without losing discriminative power), and semantic dimensionality (how many independent behaviours it moves). This post motivates that mapping programme. It doesn't propose a finished taxonomy. Understanding what a direction does matters as much as knowing how to construct it.

Appendix

A1: Mathematical formulation

Single-axis capping (Lu et al. 2026). At each capping layer, project the last-token hidden state h onto the assistant axis unit vector v:

proj = <h, v>
if proj < tau:
    h ← h + (tau - proj) · v

This pushes h along v until the projection reaches the threshold τ. When the projection is already above threshold the correction is zero, so the state passes through untouched. Otherwise the correction is exactly (τ − proj) × v, the smallest nudge that does the job.

Cross-axis capping. Two-stage gated intervention using a detection axis vd and a correction axis vc with independent thresholds:

proj_detect = <h, v_detect>
if proj_detect < tau_detect:           # assistant axis gates
    proj_correct = <h, v_correct>
    if proj_correct < tau_correct:     # compliance axis corrects
        h ← h + (tau_correct - proj_correct) · v_correct

Threshold computation. The detection threshold τd is the 1st percentile of per-layer benign (AlpacaEval) assistant-axis projections (“benign-p1”). By construction that gives a benign false-positive rate of ≤1% before the second gate fires. The correction threshold τc is the per-layer “optimal” midpoint between the refusing and compliant means on Llama, and the 75th percentile of that gap on Qwen (“optimal75”, i.e. mean_compliant + 0.75 · (mean_refusing − mean_compliant)). Both recipes were fixed before evaluation and reused unchanged across models.

Compliance axis. Built per layer across the cap band (one PC1 per capping layer) from the last-token activations of 50 refusing + 50 compliant examples. PC1 is extracted, sign-corrected so the refusal direction is positive, then L2-normalised. Cosine similarity with the assistant axis on Qwen: 0.156.

Single-axis baseline. The single-axis baseline is exactly the special case vc = vd and τc = τd of the same gate-then-correct hook body. It runs with identical hooks, thresholds, and decoding, so the only thing that changes is the corrector.

A4: Limitations

Sample size. n=250 jailbreak, n=100 benign. 95% bootstrap CIs (N=10,000 resamples): cross-axis refusal [47.5, 61.4], single-axis refusal [26.6, 39.3], paired lift [+15.1, +28.0]. Every bootstrap sample showed positive lift, so the directional claim holds even though the absolute CIs are still about 14 pp wide.

Cross-family replication. The same sign-and-magnitude lift replicates on Qwen3-32B and Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct (different families). That suggests the property split (broad detector vs. narrow corrector) reflects model geometry rather than a single-checkpoint quirk. The empirical claim is still bounded by two open-weight models, 250 WildJailbreak prompts, and one judge (Claude Sonnet 4.6).

Single threshold strategy. We didn't explore the cross-axis framework's natural advantage, which is tuning detection sensitivity and correction strength independently. Both thresholds were set using simple heuristics.

Under-determined axis. The compliance axis was computed from 100 examples (50 + 50) in a 5,120-dimensional space. PCA on this data is heavily under-determined, so the axis captures real signal mixed with noise.

Judge bias. Claude Sonnet 4.6 abstained on 12.4% of Qwen responses and 18.4% of Llama responses; these abstentions are excluded from the strict-refusal denominator. On Qwen this corresponds to 27 to 31 of the most harmful prompts the judge refused to classify. These are likely among the most successful jailbreaks, which means the true compliance rate may be higher than reported.

A6: Glossary

1. Contrast vector

A contrast vector is a direction in the model’s activation space that captures the difference between two behavioural conditions. To build one, you run the model on a set of prompts that elicit behaviour A (e.g., the model acting as a default helpful assistant) and collect the hidden-state vectors at a chosen layer for each prompt. You then do the same for behaviour B (e.g., the model role-playing a character). Each collection gives you a cloud of high-dimensional points. Take the mean of each cloud and subtract one from the other, and you get a single vector, the contrast vector, that points from the centre of B toward the centre of A.

Why does this work? If the model’s internal representation systematically shifts when it switches from one behaviour to another, that shift has a direction. The contrast vector approximates that direction. Geometrically, it is the line connecting the centroids of the two behavioural distributions. Mathematically, it approximates what a linear classifier (a probe) would learn to separate the two conditions, but it is far cheaper to compute: no gradient descent is needed, only forward passes and an averaging operation.

The quality of a contrast vector depends on the prompts used to elicit each condition. If the prompts are too similar, the vector will be noisy. If they are too different, it may capture surface-level differences (topic, length) rather than the behavioural distinction you care about. In this paper, the assistant axis is a contrast vector between “default assistant” and “role-playing” modes, while the compliance axis is a contrast vector between “refusing” and “complying with harmful requests.”

2. Forward hooks

In PyTorch (the framework used here), a forward hook is a function you register on a specific layer (module) of a neural network. Every time a tensor passes through that layer during a forward pass, the hook function is called automatically with access to the layer’s input and output. The hook can inspect these tensors, log them, or, and this is the part that matters, modify the output before it is passed to the next layer.

This is what makes real-time intervention possible. During text generation, the model processes one token at a time through all its layers. At each layer in the capping range (L46 to L53 in this experiment), a hook intercepts the hidden state, projects it onto the detection axis, checks whether the projection crosses a threshold, and if so modifies the hidden state by subtracting or adding a component along the correction axis. The modified hidden state then continues through the rest of the network as if nothing happened. But the correction shapes every subsequent computation, including which token the model ultimately selects.

Hooks are non-invasive: they do not change the model’s weights, require no retraining, and can be added or removed at any time. They run inside the model’s own forward pass, so the latency overhead is minimal, just a few vector operations per layer per token. The model itself has no mechanism to detect or resist the correction. From its perspective, the hidden state it receives at the next layer is simply different from what it would have been.

3. Hidden states

At each layer of a transformer, every token in the sequence is represented as a vector, a list of numbers (5,120 numbers for Qwen3-32B). This vector is the token’s hidden state at that layer. It encodes everything the model has computed about that token up to that point: the token’s meaning, its relationship to surrounding tokens, and the model’s emerging plan for what to generate next.

Hidden states are updated layer by layer. Each transformer layer reads the current hidden state, processes it through attention and feedforward sub-layers, and writes back an updated version. By the final layer, the hidden state encodes the model’s complete “decision” about what the next token should be. Interventions like activation capping work by modifying hidden states at intermediate layers, changing the model’s train of thought before it reaches its final conclusion.

4. Axis

In the context of neural network interpretability, an axis is a direction in the model’s high-dimensional activation space that correlates with a human-interpretable concept. The model’s hidden state at any given layer is a point in a space with thousands of dimensions. An axis is one line through that space. Project the hidden state onto this line and you get a single number, a scalar, that tells you how much of that concept is “present” in the model’s current state.

For example, the assistant axis is a direction that correlates with whether the model is in its default helpful mode. A high projection means “fully in assistant mode”; a low projection means “something has pushed the model out of its normal state.” Different axes track different concepts. The compliance axis tracks whether the model is leaning toward refusing or complying, entirely independent of what mode it is in.

5. Threshold

A threshold is the numerical boundary that turns a continuous measurement into a binary decision. In this paper, both the detection and correction stages use thresholds. The detection threshold is the 1st percentile of benign assistant-axis projections per layer (“benign-p1”), calibrated conservatively so the gate fires almost only on inputs already outside the benign distribution. The correction threshold is the per-layer optimal midpoint between the refusing and compliant means (on Llama) or the 75th percentile of that gap (on Qwen, “optimal75”). That's the point below which the model is judged to be leaning toward compliance with a harmful request.

Thresholds are calibrated per layer, because different layers encode the same concept at different scales. A projection of 0.3 at layer 46 might mean the same thing as a projection of 0.5 at layer 51. Getting thresholds wrong in either direction has consequences: too aggressive and benign prompts get incorrectly flagged (false refusals); too loose and jailbreaks slip through undetected.

6. Activation capping

Activation capping is a real-time intervention technique that enforces a ceiling on how far a model’s internal state can move along a specified direction. Here is how it works step by step:

  1. Choose a direction, a unit vector in the model’s hidden-state space. This could be the assistant axis, the compliance axis, or any other direction of interest.
  2. Choose a threshold, a minimum acceptable projection value along that direction.
  3. During generation, at each token step and at each capping layer, compute the dot product of the hidden state with the direction vector. This gives the projection, a single number measuring how far along that direction the model currently sits.
  4. If the projection is above the threshold, do nothing. The model’s state is in the acceptable range.
  5. If the projection is below the threshold, add just enough of the direction vector to the hidden state to bring the projection back up to the threshold. Mathematically: h ← h + (τ − proj) · v, where τ is the threshold and v is the direction.

The correction is minimal by construction. It changes the hidden state only as much as needed to hit the threshold, and only along the specified direction. All other components of the hidden state are left untouched. This is why capping can improve safety without destroying the model’s general capabilities: it constrains one narrow aspect of the model’s internal state while leaving everything else free.

The key design choice is which direction to cap along. Single-axis capping uses the same direction for both detecting trouble and applying the correction. Cross-axis capping separates these: it detects on one direction and corrects on a different one, so each direction can be optimised for its specific job.

7. Residual stream

The residual stream is the core data pathway in a transformer architecture. Unlike a simple pipeline where each layer completely replaces its input with its output, a transformer uses skip connections: each layer adds its output to the input it received, rather than replacing it. The result, the sum of the original input plus the layer’s contribution, is the residual stream at that point.

This means the residual stream at layer N contains the original token embedding plus the accumulated contributions of every layer from 1 to N. Attention layers add information about how tokens relate to each other; feedforward layers add nonlinear transformations of that information. Because everything is additive, the residual stream preserves information from earlier layers while allowing later layers to refine it. This is why interventions at intermediate layers can be effective: modifying the residual stream at layer 46 changes what every subsequent layer (47 through 64) sees and responds to.

8. Projection

A projection (in this context, a scalar projection) is a mathematical operation that measures how much of one vector lies along the direction of another. Given a hidden state h and a unit direction vector v, the projection is the dot product ⟨h, v⟩, a single number.

Geometrically, imagine shining a light perpendicular to v and measuring the shadow that h casts onto v. A large positive projection means h points strongly in the direction of v; a value near zero means h is mostly perpendicular to v (the concept tracked by v is largely absent); a negative value means h points away from v. In this paper, projections onto the assistant axis measure how “in-character” the model is, and projections onto the compliance axis measure how close the model is to refusing versus complying.

9. PCA

Principal Component Analysis is a dimensionality reduction technique. Given a set of data points in a high-dimensional space, PCA finds the directions along which the data varies the most. The first principal component (PC1) is the single direction that captures the maximum variance. Project all the data points onto this one line and you preserve more of the original spread than with any other line.

In this paper, PCA is applied to the hidden states of 100 prompts (50 refusing, 50 complying) collected at each capping layer, one PC1 per capping layer rather than a single layer. The resulting PC1 is the direction that best separates refusing from compliant states, and this becomes the compliance axis. We use PCA rather than a trained classifier because it requires no gradient computation and produces a single interpretable direction, which makes it cheap and transparent.

10. PC1

The first principal component, the direction that PCA identifies as carrying the most variation in the dataset. In the context of the compliance axis, PC1 captures the dominant pattern in how the model’s hidden states differ between refusing and compliant responses. It is sign-corrected so that higher values point toward refusal and lower values toward compliance, which establishes a consistent orientation for the threshold comparison.

11. Activation space

The activation space of a neural network is the high-dimensional vector space in which the model’s internal representations live. For Qwen3-32B, each hidden state is a vector with 5,120 components, so the activation space has 5,120 dimensions. Every possible state the model can be in, every possible combination of meaning, context, intent, and disposition, corresponds to a point somewhere in this space.

What makes this space useful for safety research is that it is not random. Models organise related concepts into consistent geometric structures: similar meanings cluster together, and behavioural distinctions (like refusing vs. complying) align with specific directions. This is why it is possible to find axes that track safety-relevant properties. The model has already structured its internal space in a way that makes these concepts linearly separable.
