2010–2021

Customer service

My first job was as a game programmer. After a year I had an epiphany: trading cognitive skill for money was not a proposition I enjoyed. My belief at the time was that cognitive ability is personal property, meant only for work one personally enjoys. Looking for a job that demanded minimal cognitive skill, I moved into customer service and spent nearly a decade there.

2022–2025

MSc in AI

In late 2021 I noticed people talking about AI and its potential impact on my job as a customer service executive, and decided to look into it. There was a lot of activity, most of it on Kaggle and in ML for data science. I had heard of GPT-3 and was convinced it was a PR stunt. But at the back of my mind, if it were true, it was my dream of commoditised intelligence, where human intelligence is only used for the purposes people actually want. The trend was hard to miss.

I decided to get to the bottom of it and started an MSc in AI at the University of Strathclyde in late 2022. Two months into the programme ChatGPT was released, and I knew this was not hype.

2025–2026

AI safety

I got interested in technical AI safety in early 2025. The risk is asymmetrical: the risk of absolute ruin grows exponentially with capability, which means the future of humanity depends increasingly on how technical AI safety achieves its goal. I attended a BlueDot Impact course and completed a project on persona vectors.

2026–present

Epistemic infrastructure

I now think about this differently. AI safety research is going to be automated very fast, and there is little value in being an expert on one specific research area when AI can do that research better than anyone else. The value is in making sense of the research that gets generated, and in finding directions that meaningfully reduce catastrophic risk.

Generating new research is getting cheaper, and evaluation is expected to get cheaper too. But distillation, which is understanding the implications of new research, and exposition, which is how that research augments the rest of the field and reduces existential risk, are not getting the attention they deserve, and are not being automated fast enough. Whether to automate them fully is itself a heavily debated question. I hold the strong opinion that handing frontier models the responsibility for making sure frontier models are safe is a terrible choice.

There are other epistemic aspects: the discovery and distribution of new work, and the way attention dynamics bias research effort. Certain directions get more attention because they are already legible and have a critical mass of papers and talent; others starve for lack of both: there is no existing research because there is not enough talent, and talent is discouraged because there is no ecosystem to support that kind of research.

The amount of research AI is going to generate is staggering, and we need the right epistemic infrastructure to handle that volume. I have written an article about why this matters. It is what I am currently focused on.

Now

Skimmaxxer

One example of such a tool. Skimmaxxer is a distillation tool that helps you read research papers which require understanding complex prerequisites. I use it myself and have shared it publicly.

In future I may build others to augment the epistemic infrastructure, possibly a tool for exposition: making sense of a new result, its implications, and what it enables.
